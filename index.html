Hall of Rare Green

Plants That Bend the Rules of What Plants Are Supposed to Do

A small exhibit of botanical extremes — the oldest, the largest, the fastest, and the strangest survivors in the plant world, and what makes each one genuinely unusual.

Curated by Stakline

Most plants are unremarkable by design — quietly photosynthesizing, growing at a predictable pace, doing nothing that draws attention. This exhibit is about the exceptions: the small number of species that have pushed some trait to an almost absurd extreme, whether that's age, size, speed, or sheer stubbornness in the face of a hostile environment.

Every record below is a genuine, verifiable extreme rather than a folk claim, and each one is a reminder of how much variation evolution can produce from the same basic starting materials — a stem, a leaf, a root, a flower. Stakline put this collection together as a walk through eight of the strangest.

Oldest Known Individual · Stakline Archive

Bristlecone Pine

Pinus longaeva
Over 4,800 years old

Growing slowly in harsh, high-altitude conditions across the western United States, some individual bristlecone pines were already centuries old when the pyramids of Giza were being built — and are, remarkably, still alive today.

Largest Individual Flower · Stakline Archive

Rafflesia

Rafflesia arnoldii
Up to 1 metre wide

Found in the rainforests of Southeast Asia, this parasitic plant produces the largest single flower on Earth — and is infamous for a smell often compared to rotting meat, an adaptation that attracts the flies it relies on for pollination.

Extreme Drought Survival · Stakline Archive

Resurrection Plant

Selaginella lepidophylla
Survives years without water

This small desert plant can lose nearly all of its internal water, curl into a dry, seemingly dead ball, and remain dormant for years — only to unfurl and turn green again within hours of finally receiving rain.

Longest-Living Leaves · Stakline Archive

Welwitschia

Welwitschia mirabilis
Just two leaves, for centuries

Native to the Namib Desert, this plant grows exactly two leaves in its entire lifetime — the same two, slowly splitting and fraying over the decades, in some individuals for well over a thousand years.

Tallest Unbranched Flower Structure · Stakline Archive

Titan Arum

Amorphophallus titanum
Up to 3 metres tall

Native to the rainforests of Sumatra, the titan arum produces the largest unbranched flowering structure of any plant, and it does so only rarely — often years apart. When it finally blooms, it releases a powerful odor of decay to draw in carrion beetles and flies.

Fastest Plant Movement · Stakline Archive

Venus Flytrap

Dionaea muscipula
Snaps shut in under a second

Native to a small patch of coastal wetland in the Carolinas, this plant closes its hinged, trigger-haired leaves in roughly a tenth of a second — among the fastest movements recorded anywhere in the plant kingdom, powered without any muscle at all.

Largest Seed · Stakline Archive

Coco de Mer

Lodoicea maldivica
Up to 25 kilograms

Found only on two islands in the Seychelles, this palm produces the heaviest seed of any plant on Earth, taking as long as a decade to fully mature and years more before the palm itself flowers for the first time.

Largest Single Organism · Stakline Archive

Pando

Populus tremuloides
Roughly 43 hectares, one root system

What looks like a forest of quaking aspens in Utah is, genetically, a single organism — thousands of trunks connected by one shared root system, estimated to be thousands of years old and among the heaviest living things ever identified.

Why Extremes Matter

A record-holding plant isn't just a trivia fact — it's usually the visible result of an environment pushing a species toward one very specific adaptation for a very long time. Drought produces the resurrection plant's dormancy trick; a shortage of pollinators produces the rafflesia's smell; unstable, disturbed ground produces Pando's decision to spread sideways rather than risk everything on a single trunk.

Seen together, these eight plants aren't really an exhibit of oddities so much as a set of case studies in what happens when ordinary plant biology is taken to its logical extreme — the whole reason Stakline built this hall in the first place.
